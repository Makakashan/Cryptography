In the study of classical cryptography students often begin with substitution ciphers and then move toward modern stream ciphers and one time pads The one time pad is famous because in theory it offers perfect secrecy when used correctly A random key as long as the message must be used exactly once and then destroyed If any of these rules are broken security can collapse very quickly

This demonstration text is prepared for an educational exercise about key reuse Imagine that several independent messages are encrypted with the same pad segment Each ciphertext alone may appear random but relations between ciphertexts reveal relations between plaintexts In particular the xor of two ciphertexts equals the xor of the two original messages and that leaks structure immediately

Human language has redundancy Spaces are frequent letters follow patterns and common words repeat When attackers combine many ciphertext lines of equal length they can guess likely space positions and recover parts of the secret key Once some key bytes are known they can decrypt corresponding positions in every line and expand their knowledge iteratively This process does not always recover everything but it often recovers enough to expose sensitive meaning

For this reason secure systems never reuse one time pad material Modern protocols also avoid nonce reuse in stream encryption because the mathematical danger is similar Reusing keystream turns strong encryption into a brittle scheme vulnerable to statistical and structural attacks The lesson is simple operational discipline matters as much as elegant theory in practical cryptography
